01The theory

The Optimistic Theory.

Every interface you love lies to you a little. It fills the heart, moves the card, and marks the message sent before any server has agreed. Engineers call it an optimistic update, and it works because belief, followed by verification, is faster than doubt followed by proof.

I run the same pattern outside the screen. On failing products, on under-resourced teams, on companies that stopped believing their own roadmap. Four laws, learned the expensive way. Keep scrolling; they'll introduce themselves.

Law 01

Believe first.

Nobody at Yubi believed Aspero could be saved: ₹18 crore in losses, under 300 customers. I treated the better version as already real and worked backwards from it. Optimism, then evidence. In that order.

Reconciled: ₹860M revenue, 18 months later
Law 02

One warm pixel.

Keep everything disciplined, monochrome, precise. Then place one warm point of belief in the frame, and protect it. On this site it's a literal orange pixel. In a company, it's usually a person. I've been that pixel twice, and I've hired twenty more.

Reconciled: 94% of the org stayed
Law 03

Reality must catch up.

Optimism without receipts is delusion. Every belief eventually reconciles against the server. So I price feelings: I put a number on Yubi's design chaos and showed the CPTO the invoice instead of asking for budget. That conversation happened once.

Reconciled: ₹186Cr saved in FY25, confirmed by finance
Law 04

Rollback without shame.

Optimistic systems fail gracefully: when reality disagrees, revert fast, learn, retry. I rolled back an engineering career to become a designer. I rolled myself out of Yubi at the top, because the org I built didn't need me anymore. Both felt like failure for a week and freedom after.

Reconciled: both practices outlasted me
02The origin

The question that cost me a career.

I started as a developer, and I was good at it. But I kept asking a question that wasn't in any brief: does this feel right to the person using it? In 2010 I walked into a design school and didn't look back.

That question cost me a perfectly fine engineering career. Worth it. The craft took years. The conviction took one afternoon at a keyboard. Everything since, two design organisations built from scratch, a $140M ecosystem rebuilt, a failing product turned into the company's proudest number, started with believing an answer before I could prove it.
