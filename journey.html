My journey · 14 years · 4 companies

14 years. Two orgs built from scratch.

From writing code at NIC to directing design across a $140M fintech ecosystem. The full story, including the failures, the one-meeting wins, and the moment a product nobody believed in became the most important one in the portfolio.

Chapter I · Where it started

I started as a developer. I was good at it.

But I kept asking a question that wasn't in any brief I was given: does this feel right to the person using it?

That question cost me a perfectly fine engineering career. Worth it.

In 2010 I walked into ICAT School of Design. Not because design was a better career move. Because I finally admitted that it was the thing I'd been trying to do with code all along.

The craft took years. The conviction took one afternoon at a keyboard.

Chapter II · The first build

One designer. Five hundred engineers. No brief.

In 2015 I joined HashedIn as their first designer. Nobody believed design mattered, not yet. But the founders trusted me before I trusted myself, and that's a particular kind of pressure. The kind that makes you build something real or break trying.

I didn't break.

Four years later, HashedIn had a 32-person design practice: its own vertical, its own standards, its own identity inside the company. When Deloitte acquired HashedIn in 2021, that practice went with it.

"You can change what a company believes about design, but only if you're willing to go first."

Chapter III · The second build, bigger stakes

Five products. No system. Beautiful, expensive chaos.

Yubi in 2022 was a different kind of challenge. Not one company finding design, but five product brands losing their way without it. A $140 million ecosystem. Twenty designers who were talented and exhausted. No shared language. No north star.

Maybe you know this feeling. You've built something real: users, revenue, a team. And somewhere in the speed of it, design became a function that ships things rather than shapes them. That's not a design problem. That's a leadership problem.

It's the problem I was hired to fix.

Chapter IV · The turnaround

₹18 crore in losses. Under 300 customers. Broken trust.

The first product I touched at Yubi was Aspero. A retail bond investment platform that had every reason to work and somehow didn't. Under 300 active investors. ₹18 crore in losses. A brand that communicated complexity when it needed to communicate calm.

Nobody at Yubi believed Aspero could be saved. That made it easier, in a way. When the expectations are low enough, you're free to be honest about what's actually broken.

We didn't redesign it. We reimagined what it was trying to do, and for whom. The onboarding became a guided story. The product started performing trust instead of performing complexity.

Eighteen months later: 20,000 active investors. ₹860 million in revenue. 32% of Yubi's total.

"It's not about making things beautiful. It's about making people believe something is possible before they have proof that it is."
Chapter V · The system

44 designers. 13 products. Zero common language.

Every inconsistency was a conversation that hadn't happened. Every rework was a cost nobody had calculated. So I calculated it. ₹136 crore: the cost of design fragmentation in engineering rework over three years.

I didn't ask for a design system budget. I showed the CPTO the invoice.

That conversation happened once.

Bugs dropped from 139 to 4. Adoption went from 30% to 85%. The engineering team went from 68 front-end engineers to 12. The NPS went from 1.7 to 8.23. For the first time, Yubi felt like it was made by one team.
