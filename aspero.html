01Business situation

The honest state
of Aspero when
I arrived.

Aspero was Yubi's retail bond investment platform. On paper, it had every reason to work. In reality, it was losing money, losing users, and losing faith internally.

When I joined Yubi in 2022, Aspero had been live for over a year. The product existed in a category with real market demand — retail bond investing was an underserved space in India. The business case was there. The team was capable. But the numbers told a different story: under 300 active investors, ₹18 crore in losses, and a product that most internal stakeholders had quietly written off.

The deeper problem wasn't acquisition. It was that Aspero couldn't hold users once they arrived. First-time retail bond investors — people who'd never bought a financial instrument beyond an FD — were landing on a platform that assumed they already knew what they were doing. The onboarding asked for information without explaining why. The bond listings displayed data without explaining value. The brand communicated complexity instead of calm.

This wasn't a feature problem. It was a trust problem — and trust is a design problem.

The business context

👤

02Design strategy

The diagnosis came
before the design.

Most failing products get redesigned when what they need is re-thought. Before touching a single screen, I spent four weeks on diagnosis. The findings changed everything about how we approached the solution.

The audit was structured around three questions: What is the product actually communicating right now? What do users need to feel before they'll invest? What is the gap between those two things?

Session recordings, user interviews, analytics deep-dives, and a full heuristic audit of every screen. What I found was that Aspero had been designed from the inside out — built for people who already understood bonds, not for people deciding whether to trust the platform with their money.

"We didn't have a design problem. We had a communication problem — and the design was doing the communicating. We had to change what it said before we changed how it looked."

The north star question I gave the team: "Why would a first-time retail bond investor trust this platform enough to put their savings in?" Every design decision for the next 18 months was filtered through that question.

The strategic bets we made: Rebuild onboarding as a guided story, not a form. Restructure bond listings to communicate value before data. Redesign the brand to perform calm and confidence. Reduce cognitive load at every step. Make the product feel like it was built for the user, not for the regulator.

03Brand & design language

Every pixel was
performing trust.

The Aspero rebrand wasn't a visual exercise. It was a communication strategy. Every design decision — colour, copy, hierarchy, motion — was asking one question: does this make a first-time investor feel safe?

When I inherited Aspero, the brand was functionally invisible. It looked like every other fintech product — cool blues, angular typography, data-forward layouts that told the user what was available rather than how to feel about it. Fine for an institutional audience. Alienating for a first-time retail investor who'd never bought a bond in their life.

The first brand decision was colour. We chose green — deliberately, and for reasons that go beyond aesthetics. Green carries a specific psychological load in the context of Indian retail investing: it is the colour of growth, of positive returns, of money moving in the right direction. It's also the colour most Indians associate with financial safety. In a category defined by anxiety, green was a deliberate de-escalation.

The second decision was what to lead with. The hero line — "Invest & earn upto 15.00% returns" — puts the outcome first. Not the instrument, not the mechanism, not a regulatory label. The return. Because that's the question every potential investor is actually asking. We answered it before they had to ask.

Payout confirmation — closing the loop with total transparency

The payout confirmation modal is the most important screen in the product that nobody ever talks about.

A first-time investor's greatest anxiety isn't whether the platform works while they're investing. It's whether it works when they need their money back. The payout modal exists entirely to dissolve that anxiety — transparently, at the exact moment it matters most.

The design decisions here are deliberate: the Yubi U logo appears (institutional credibility — this is backed by Yubi, a $140M ecosystem), the breakdown is line by line (Principal / TDS / Receivable — no black box), and the primary CTA is confirmatory ("Yes, I have received") not dismissive. The product is asking for closure, not just confirmation. That's a trust relationship, not a transaction.

This modal exists because we discovered in research that investors who hadn't received payout confirmations were generating support tickets and — more critically — not reinvesting. Closing the payout loop through the app increased reinvestment rates and materially reduced support volume.

I made three deliberate team decisions that shaped the outcome:

01
Researcher embedded from day one — not brought in to validate decisions already made. We had a researcher running studies before we had designs to test. This changed the quality of our strategic decisions significantly.
02
Content strategy as a design function — UX writing on Aspero wasn't a polish step. The words were doing structural work — building trust, reducing anxiety, explaining value. I treated the content strategist as a core design voice, not a support role.
03
I stayed in the work — at Director level, there's pressure to manage up and leave execution to the team. On Aspero, I made a deliberate choice to stay close to the design decisions. The stakes were too high for the typical "approve the mockup" model.
05Plan & workflow

How 18 months of
work was structured.

The work ran in three phases. Each phase had a clear goal, a defined team structure, and measurable exit criteria. We didn't move to the next phase until the current one had landed.

Phase 1 · Weeks 1–6
Diagnostic & strategic alignment
Full product audit. 12 user interviews with first-time retail investors. Analytics deep-dive. Stakeholder workshops with CEO, CFO, and CPO to align on north star and what success would look like in 6 months. Key output: a shared understanding that this was a trust problem, not a feature gap. This was the most important 6 weeks. Getting alignment on diagnosis before solutions prevented scope creep and political friction later.
User research synthesis Design audit report North star definition Stakeholder alignment deck Success metrics framework
Phase 2 · Weeks 7–20
Onboarding & trust architecture
The onboarding flow was rebuilt from scratch. We reduced KYC from 11 steps to 5. Every step was redesigned with a single principle: the product should feel like a knowledgeable friend helping you invest, not a regulatory form you're filling in. We ran 4 rounds of usability testing. I personally reviewed every screen against the north star question before any handoff. The brand refresh ran in parallel — we needed the visual language to carry the same trust signals as the UX.
New onboarding flow KYC redesign (11→5 steps) Brand refresh Usability testing ×4 Design system tokens for Aspero
Phase 3 · Weeks 21–44
Bond listings, discovery & retention
The listing pages were the second major failure point. Users who made it through onboarding were dropping off when they hit the bond marketplace. We rebuilt the bond card system to lead with value (yield, safety rating, tenure) before data. We introduced contextual education — small, in-context explanations of financial concepts, never intrusive. Portfolio view and post-investment communication were redesigned to reinforce the feeling that their money was being looked after. The retention numbers shifted significantly in Q3 2023.

06Key decisions

The calls that
defined the outcome.

Every project has decisions that shape the result. These are the ones that mattered — including the ones where I pushed back against what the business wanted.

Decision 01 — Strategic
Strategic
Should we launch new features or fix the existing experience?
The business pressure was to add features — more bond types, comparison tools, market data feeds. I pushed back. The user research was unambiguous: the existing experience was broken before new features would help anyone. We negotiated a 6-week freeze on new feature development to fix the foundation. This was the hardest conversation I had with the CPO. I won it with data, not opinion.
Why it mattered: if we'd launched features on a broken experience, we'd have accelerated the wrong thing.
Decision 02 — Design
Design
How much financial education belongs in the product?
The marketing team wanted extensive education sections — explainer articles, video content, a full learning centre. My position: education at the point of decision, nowhere else. Contextual tooltips, in-card explanations, step-by-step guides that appear only when the user is about to do something they don't understand. Education as a destination is ignored. Education as ambient context gets used.
Why it mattered: users don't read. They do. We had to meet them in the doing, not the reading.
Decision 03 — Brand
Brand
Should Aspero feel like a Yubi product or a standalone brand?
Aspero's target user (first-time retail investor) had a different mental model than Yubi's core institutional users. I argued for a distinct brand expression — same Yubi values, but a warmer, more human visual language that wouldn't feel institutional and cold to a retail audience. The Aspero brand was a sub-expression of Yubi, not a copy of it.
Why it mattered: trust signals are context-dependent. What builds trust with a CFO doesn't build trust with a first-time investor.
Decision 04 — Process
Process
How do we prevent engineering from shipping ahead of design completion?
We established a design sign-off protocol for Aspero — no screen goes to development without a completed design review against the north star framework. This wasn't popular with the engineering team in the short term. In practice, it prevented regression and saved us from shipping inconsistent experiences. The engineering lead eventually became one of its strongest advocates.
Why it mattered: the trust architecture breaks if any screen breaks it. Consistency isn't optional when trust is the product.

08What I learned

The honest
retrospective.

Aspero was the project that taught me the most about the gap between good design thinking and good design leadership. These are the things I'd do the same — and the things I'd do differently.

✓
What worked: diagnostic before design
The 6-week freeze on design output at the start — spending time on diagnosis and alignment — paid back tenfold. Every hour spent on research and stakeholder alignment before designing saved multiple hours of design rework and political friction later.
✓
What worked: content as a core design function
Embedding a content strategist from phase one changed the quality of the product. Financial products communicate through language as much as layout. The words were doing structural work — and treating them as a design decision rather than a copy task made a measurable difference.
△
What I'd do differently: earlier A/B testing infrastructure
We built A/B testing capability in Phase 3. It should have been in place from Phase 2. Earlier quantitative data would have given us more confidence in some of the bigger UX bets we were making on instinct and qualitative research alone.
△
What I'd do differently: faster brand decision
The brand direction debate with marketing took too long. In hindsight, I should have pushed for a decision in week 2, not week 8. The brand underpins everything — every week of ambiguity was a week of design decisions made on uncertain foundations.
What wouldn't have happened without this design leadership approach
🔍
The feature freeze. Without a design leader willing to push back against scope expansion, we'd have shipped new features on a broken foundation and made the problem worse.
📐
The trust architecture. Framing the problem as a trust deficit — not a UX problem or a feature gap — changed every subsequent decision. That reframing came from design leadership, not from product or engineering.
👥
The team structure. Researcher and content strategist embedded from day one wasn't the default. I made the case to the CPO that good research and good writing were not optional on a financial trust product. That argument changed the outcome.
🎯
The north star question. "Why would a first-time retail bond investor trust this platform with their savings?" — that question, and the discipline to filter every decision through it, kept 18 months of work aligned without constant re-explanation.
